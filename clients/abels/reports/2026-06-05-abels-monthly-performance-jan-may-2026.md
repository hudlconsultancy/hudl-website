## 2. Website (GA4) — Jan to May 2026

Source: GA4 daily export aggregated to months. Sessions and new users are additive and reliable monthly. **Unique active users** can't be cleanly month-split from a daily export (a visitor active on several days is counted each day), so the table shows session-based traffic as the headline; period total unique active users = **12,633** (1 Jan–4 Jun).

| **Jan** | 3,943 | 3,187 | 441 | 42.6s | 0.61 | 2.76% |

| **Mar** | 2,814 | 2,156 | 391 | 48.2s | 0.64 | 3.27% |

| **Total (Jan–May)** | **15,341** | **12,061** | — | **~44s** | — | **~4.0%** |

*(1–4 June partial: 424 sessions, 343 new users.)*

**What this shows:**
- **Traffic eased from a January peak** (3,943 sessions) to a March–May plateau of ~2,600–2,850/mo. This tracks the PMAX pause (less paid display traffic) and the strategic shift away from soft-action volume — fewer low-intent visits, not a collapse.
- **Engagement quality held or improved** — average engagement time stayed in the 43–48s band and **May's engaged-sessions-per-user jumped to 1.05** (the strongest of the period), suggesting the traffic that remained was more engaged.
- **Key-event rate is volatile** (2.8%–6.3%) — February spiked to 6.3%. Worth confirming which key events GA4 is counting, as this drives the on-site conversion read.
- **New users dominate** (~80%+ of sessions) — a low returning-visitor base, normal for a one-off, high-consideration purchase like a house move.

**Website data gap:** the 2026 GA4 export has **no channel breakdown** (organic vs paid vs direct vs referral). We can see total traffic but not how much came from PPC vs SEO vs direct. The 2025 files *do* carry a channel split — a matching 2026 channel export would let us attribute this traffic and complete the SEO picture below.
